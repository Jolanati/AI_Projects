  • (~40 s) Two possible mistakes, very different prices. A missed fraud is real money lost (average $518 in our data) plus a chargeback and lost trust. A false alarm is an annoyed customer and a phone call.
  • So our goal is: catch as much fraud as possible, while keeping false alarms low. "When in doubt, stop the transaction."
  • Three users: the cardholder, the fraud analyst who reviews cases, and the bank that pays for losses.

  • (~45 s) Why do we need AI at all? We tested the obvious rules on exactly the same test payments as the model.
  • "Block every payment over $500" catches 152 of 356 frauds (43%) and blocks 637 innocent customers. "Block night payments over $300" catches 185 (52%) with 287 false alarms.

  • And fraud changes: rules must be rewritten by hand, a model is retrained. It also gives a score, so unsure cases can go to a person.

For a real bank

Its own payments

Retrain on the bank's data, with fraud labels from chargebacks.

  • (~50 s) Our data: a public Kaggle dataset, 339,607 card payments from 13 western US states, 2019–2020. Lecture 2: an existing dataset is cheap and ready today, but it was collected by someone else for someone else.
  • The core challenge: only 1,782 frauds, 1 in 191. A model hardly sees any fraud to learn from.
  • Our fix: generated data. SMOTE creates new fraud examples by blending similar real frauds, until the training data is 50/50: 268,833 synthetic frauds made from 1,426 real ones. We also tested the alternative, class weights, where rare fraud simply counts more.
  • The test set (67,922 payments) stays 100% real and untouched, so every result we show is on real payments.
  • For a real bank: retrain on its own payments; the fraud label then comes from chargebacks weeks later. Check whether the Kaggle data is simulated, and say so if asked.

  • Night: 86% of fraud happens between 22:00 and 04:00, a window with only 23% of payments. Amount: fraud averages $518 vs $68. Category: online shopping and grocery have the highest fraud rates. Age: customers over 60 are targeted twice as often as 30–45 year olds.

  • (~50 s) Lecture 3: start simple, change one idea at a time. We trained five models, from a simple straight-line model to tree ensembles, tuned each with cross-validation and judged all on the same locked test set.
  • Baseline (Logistic Regression) caught 237 frauds with 641 false alarms. The champion, LightGBM, caught 299 (84%) with only 24 false alarms.
  • We chose on balance, not on the single highest number: XGBoost caught 13 more but with 47 false alarms. Honest note: with only 356 frauds, a gap of 13 is within the noise (about ±14), so it's a business choice to confirm on live data.
  • The model outputs a score. Low: approve. High (0.4 and up): block. In between (0.1–0.4): a fraud analyst decides. On our test set that middle band holds only about 23 payments, 6 of them fraud.
  • Details in the backup deck: experiment log, threshold tuning, and the lesson that synthetic data made validation look too good.

  • (~50 s) Launch (Lecture 8): first shadow mode, where the model scores real payments but blocks nothing. Fraud is confirmed by chargebacks up to ~60 days later, so we wait before judging. Then a canary release: 5%, 25%, 100% of payments, always with a way back.
