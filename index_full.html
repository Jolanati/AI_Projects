Part 1 · Problem and users

Stop fraud before the customer loses money

A card issuer in the western US wants to block fraudulent payments automatically.

Cardholder

Wants to pay without friction

and never to lose money to a stolen card.

Fraud analyst

Reviews suspicious payments

Needs few, well-chosen cases, not thousands of alerts.

The bank

Pays for every missed fraud

Chargebacks, lost trust, and regulators watching.

Missed fraud

Fraud → approved: expensive

The money is gone. Average fraud payment in our data: $518.

False alarm

Legit → blocked: recoverable

The customer calls, the card is unblocked in minutes.

Why AI and not fixed rules? Fraud is a combination: late hour + large amount + online purchase + customer profile. Rules miss combinations and fraudsters learn them. A model learns from history and can be retrained.
  • Three users with different needs: the cardholder (no friction, no loss), the fraud analyst (manageable workload), the bank (losses and trust).
  • The two mistakes have very different prices. Lecture 6: "a wrong blur is cheap; a missed face is not." Ours: a false alarm is cheap, a missed fraud is not.
  • Build or buy: no ready-made public model fits one bank's fraud patterns, so we train our own, with free tools.

  • Lecture 2, four data sources: we chose an existing dataset. Cost low, speed "ready today", but fit is often poor: it is not our bank, our customers or our time period.
  • The challenge is imbalance: 339,607 payments, but only 1,782 frauds. Lecture 2, "plan the variety, not the number": those few cases decide how good the model can get.
  • Fixing the imbalance with generated data (Lecture 2's fourth source: "made by a program; cheap, may not look real"). SMOTE creates a new fraud example on the line between a real fraud and one of its 5 most similar frauds. The training set went from 1,426 frauds among 271,685 payments to a 50/50 balance: 270,259 legitimate and 270,259 fraud, so 268,833 fraud examples are synthetic.
  • Only the training data was balanced. The test set stays 100% real, so the final scores reflect real payments.
  • We tested two approaches to imbalance: SMOTE for Logistic Regression, Decision Tree and Random Forest; class weights (rare fraud counts more, no generated data) for XGBoost and LightGBM. Part 5 shows what that taught us.
  • Labels: here the fraud column is given. In production, the answer comes weeks later through chargebacks (Lecture 8).
  • Before any analysis, 20% (67,922 payments) was locked away as the test set, stratified to keep the 0.52% fraud ratio, with random seed 42 so it is reproducible.
  • Check whether the Kaggle data is simulated, and say so if it is.

15 raw columns → 39 model inputs.

  • Exploration before modelling: we looked for what makes fraud different, then turned each finding into a signal (feature). All numbers here are computed from the full dataset.
  • Night: 86% of fraud happens between 22:00 and 03:59, although only 23% of all payments happen then. Fraud rate at night 1.92% vs 0.10% in the daytime, about 19× higher.
  • Amount: average fraud $518 vs $68 for legitimate payments (medians $356 vs $46). 47% of frauds are above $500, and about 1 in 5 payments above $500 is fraud. Log amount compresses the huge range.
  • Category: online shopping 1.44%, in-store grocery 1.32%, online misc 1.28%, against 0.15% for home goods. One yes/no column per category (one-hot encoding).
  • Age: customers over 60 have a fraud rate of 0.72%, double the 0.35% of 30–45 year olds.
  • Tested assumption: we expected fraud far from the cardholder's home. The data says no: average distance is 75.6 km for both. We kept the feature, but this is a project lesson: test assumptions with data instead of trusting intuition.

Part 5 · Building and testing

How we tested and chose the model

How we test

Same data and features for every model. Final score only on the locked test set: 356 frauds in 67,922 payments.

How we tune

Settings searched automatically, checked with 5-fold cross-validation on training data only.

How we choose

Against the part-4 criteria: best balance of fraud caught vs false alarms (F1). Start simple; one new idea per run.

RunIdea testedFrauds caughtFalse alarmsTest F1Decision

Champion: LightGBM, not the top catcher. XGBoost caught 13 more frauds but raised twice the false alarms. LightGBM has the best balance (highest F1, ROC-AUC 0.998). Losing runs stay on record.
  • Lecture 3: every run gets a row (idea, change, result, decision), and we change one thing at a time.
  • From baseline to champion: 237 → 299 frauds caught (Recall 67% → 84%), false alarms 641 → 24.
  • Fair experiment: same split, same features, tuned on F1 with 5-fold cross-validation, judged on the untouched test set.
  • The champion choice is a recorded trade-off, not "highest number wins". Lecture 3 champion loop: losers stay on record.

Tune the alarm, then question the result

Recall, precision and F1 by threshold

LightGBM, test set

0.5 → 0.4: +5 frauds caught (304) for +4 false alarms (28). Highest F1: 0.884.

We hit this

Generated data made validation look too good

Synthetic fraud (SMOTE) was created before cross-validation, so copies leaked into the check.

Random Forest
CV 0.999 → test 0.768
LightGBM
CV 0.873 → test 0.881
We nearly fell for this

Wins smaller than the noise

With 356 frauds, 84% Recall is uncertain by ±4 points (≈ ±14 frauds). Our two “wins” are 13 and 5 frauds.

Lesson: trust only the locked test set, and treat small gaps as business choices to confirm on live data.

  • Threshold: the model outputs a probability; the business sets the cutoff. Like a smoke alarm's sensitivity. Optimisation without retraining.
  • Lecture 2 warns that generated data "may not look real". SMOTE is generated data: models trained on it scored near-perfect in cross-validation (RF 0.999, DT 0.998, LR 0.979) but much lower on the test set. Class-weighted models (XGBoost, LightGBM) had CV ≈ test. Fix: generate synthetic examples inside each fold.
  • Lecture 3 "measure the noise" and Lecture 8 "same AI, three tests, three scores": 95% margin on Recall with 356 cases is about ±3.8 points. LightGBM vs XGBoost (13 frauds) and 0.4 vs 0.5 (5 frauds) sit inside it.
  • The final choice still holds, because it was judged on the untouched test set and on the business trade-off.

Part 8 · Risks

What could go wrong, and what we'd do

Fraudsters adapt

Drift

New fraud patterns appear; the model ages. Do: drift alerts, regular retraining.

Customers hurt

Wrong blocks

A real purchase declined abroad or at night. Do: review band, fast unblock, track complaints.

Unequal errors

A group fares worse

Age is a top-2 feature: older customers may be blocked more. Do: results per group.

Late answers

Labels arrive weeks later

We learn about mistakes only after chargebacks. Do: wait before judging, audit sample.

Personal data

Privacy (GDPR)

Birth date, job, home location. Do: access by role, deletion on request, data in the EU.

One bad input

Errors spread

A wrong birth date or address silently corrupts age and distance. Do: input checks, fall back to rules.

  • Drift is guaranteed in fraud (Lecture 8: Instacart in 2020). Fraudsters react to what gets blocked.
  • Fairness: age is the model's #2 feature. That may be a real fraud signal, but we must check error rates per age group (Lecture 8: averages that hide a group).
  • Lecture 2: personal data needs an owner, access by role, and deletion "from every version, following a written process".
  • Lecture 6: one wrong answer spreads to many steps. A bad customer profile breaks several features at once.
  • From our own project: generated data and small samples can mislead (part 5).
