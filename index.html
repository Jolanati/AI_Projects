  • (~20 s) "Imagine a fraud detector that is 99.5% accurate. Sounds great? It catches zero fraud."
  • Only about 0.5% of card payments are fraud, so a model that always says "not fraud" is right 99.5% of the time, and useless.
  • "We are the AI team at NordPay, a Baltic card issuer. In the next minutes we show you how our AI catches fraud every day: our data, why it needs AI, how we built it, and how it runs."

The setting. NordPay is a fictional Baltic card issuer. The process we show (daily data, real-time scoring, analyst reviews, nightly retraining by an AI agent) is how a modern company would run fraud detection. The model results we quote were measured on our prototype dataset, which is said once on the data slide.

Why accuracy misleads here. In our prototype data only 1,782 of 339,607 payments are fraud (0.52%). A model that answers "not fraud" every time is correct 99.48% of the time, yet never stops a single fraudster. Accuracy counts every correct answer equally, so when one class is tiny the big class dominates the score.

What we measure instead. Recall (the share of real fraud we catch), false alarms as a guardrail (we must not block every customer), and money as the business measure. Every number later in the pitch is one of these three.

  • (~40 s) Two possible mistakes with very different prices. A missed fraud is real money lost (on average $518) plus a chargeback and lost trust. A false alarm is a genuine payment held until an analyst releases it.
  • So our goal: catch as much fraud as possible, while keeping false alarms low. "When in doubt, stop the payment."
  • Three users: the cardholder, the fraud analyst who reviews cases, and NordPay, which pays for the losses.

Two kinds of mistakes. A missed fraud (false negative) means a fraudulent payment is approved. A false alarm (false positive) means a genuine payment is blocked. A fraud model always trades one against the other: catching more fraud usually means more false alarms.

Why the prices differ. In our prototype data the average fraudulent payment is $518 (median $356), against $68 for a normal payment. On top of the lost money there is the chargeback process and the risk of losing the customer. A false alarm costs a moment of friction, which is recoverable, but too many of them drive customers away, so false alarms are a guardrail.

Course link. Lecture 6: "a wrong blur is cheap; a missed face is not." This cost difference drives every later decision: the metric, the model choice, the threshold and the manual review step.

  • (~45 s) Why do we need AI at all? We tested the obvious rules on exactly the same test payments as the model.
  • "Block every payment over $500" catches 152 of 356 frauds (43%) and blocks 637 innocent customers. "Block night payments over $300" catches 185 (52%) with 287 false alarms.
  • Our AI model catches 299 (84%) with only 24 false alarms. It combines many weak signals that a rule looks at one by one.
  • And fraud changes: rules are rewritten by hand, our model is retrained every night. It also gives a score, so suspicious payments go to an analyst.

How we tested the rules. Both rules were applied to the same locked test set of our prototype dataset as the model: 67,922 payments, 356 of them fraud. The night window is 22:00–03:59.

Why the model wins. A rule looks at one or two signals and draws a hard line. Fraud rarely shows up in one signal; it shows up in combinations, for example a mid-sized online purchase late at night by an older customer. The model weighs all inputs together and learns these combinations from past payments.

Why it matters over time. Fraudsters adapt to whatever gets blocked. A rule set has to be rewritten by hand; a model is retrained on newly confirmed fraud. The score from 0 to 1 also lets NordPay treat payments differently: low-risk payments pass smoothly, flagged ones go to an analyst for review.

Honest caveat. These are simple illustrative rules, not a tuned rule engine. Real rule sets are stronger, but they hit the same limit: every rule is written by a person and checks a fixed combination.

  • (~50 s) Our model is fed by five sources. The payment system streams every payment live; customer, merchant and card-network data refresh daily. In the demo you will see the payment stream and the feedback labels; the other sources are the production plan.
  • The fraud answers are the labels the model learns from. They arrive with delays: analyst reviews the same day, customer complaints within days, chargebacks after up to 60 days.
  • Every night the data is checked and saved as a dated version, so we always know which data trained which model.

Live vs daily. Payment data must be live because the model scores each payment while the customer waits. Customer, merchant and network data change slowly, so a nightly refresh is enough. Lecture 2: information saved at capture costs nothing; added a month later it costs days, so every payment is stored with its score, model version and decision.

Labels with delays. Fraud is only confirmed later. Analyst reviews of flagged payments give same-day labels, customer complaints about approved payments reveal missed fraud within days, and chargebacks arrive after 30–60 days. The model can only be judged and retrained on payments whose answers have arrived (Lecture 8: who holds the answer sheet, and when).

The nightly job. Load the day's payments, run data checks (missing fields, changed formats, unusual volumes; a failed check stops the run and alerts the data engineer), refresh customer profiles, attach new fraud answers, save a dated dataset version (Lecture 2: which exact data trained the model?).

The prototype dataset. A public dataset with the same structure as card-payment data: 339,607 payments, 2019–2020, 15 columns, no missing values. 20% was locked away as the test set (67,922 payments, 356 frauds), stratified and with a fixed seed. The training set (271,685 payments, 1,426 frauds) was balanced with SMOTE: each synthetic fraud is placed between a real fraud and one of its five most similar frauds, giving 270,259 of each class. XGBoost and LightGBM used class weights instead. If asked: the prototype is a public dataset, because a student team cannot use real customers' data.

  • (~45 s) Before building any model, we looked at what makes fraud different, and turned each finding into a signal.
  • Night: 86% of fraud happens between 22:00 and 04:00. Amount: fraud averages $518 vs $68. Online shopping and grocery have the highest fraud rates. Customers over 60 are targeted twice as often as 30–45 year olds.
  • We also tested "fraud happens far from home": the data said no. Lesson: test assumptions with data.
  • In production we add behaviour signals our live data gives us: how many payments in the last hour, a new device, spending far above the customer's usual.

From pattern to signal. This step is called feature engineering: the 15 raw columns of the prototype data became 39 model inputs. All numbers on this slide are computed from the full prototype dataset.

The findings. Night: fraud rate 1.92% at night vs 0.10% in the daytime, about 19 times higher. Amount: medians $356 vs $46; 47% of frauds are above $500; the model also uses the logarithm of the amount to tame the range up to $28,000. Category: online shopping 1.44%, in-store grocery 1.32%, online miscellaneous 1.28% vs 0.15% for home goods. Age: 0.72% for customers over 60 vs 0.35% for 30–45 year olds.

The failed assumption. We calculated the distance between home and merchant for every payment: 75.6 km on average for both fraud and normal payments. We kept the feature, but the lesson is to test intuition with data.

Added in production. Live data gives behaviour signals the prototype data does not have: velocity (payments per card in the last hour), a new device or new country, spending compared with this customer's usual pattern, and the merchant's recent fraud rate. These are the strongest signals in modern fraud detection, because most fraud today is online and mobile, where a stolen card behaves differently from its owner. They are part of the NordPay plan, not of our prototype results.

  • (~50 s) Lecture 3: start simple, change one idea at a time. We trained five models, from a simple straight-line model to tree ensembles, and judged all on the same locked test set.
  • The baseline caught 237 frauds with 641 false alarms. The champion, LightGBM, caught 299 (84%) with only 24 false alarms.
  • We chose on balance: XGBoost caught 13 more, but with twice the false alarms.
  • The model gives a fraud probability: below 0.5 the payment is approved, at 0.5 and above it is flagged as fraud and reviewed by an analyst.

The approach (Lecture 3). Logistic Regression tests a single straight-line rule; Decision Tree tests yes/no rule combinations; Random Forest a vote of many trees; XGBoost trees that correct each other's mistakes; LightGBM the same idea, built for speed. Each model was tuned automatically with 5-fold cross-validation on the training data only, then judged once on the locked test set of our prototype dataset.

Results (Recall / precision / F1): Logistic Regression 0.67 / 0.27 / 0.38; Decision Tree 0.77 / 0.63 / 0.70; Random Forest 0.70 / 0.85 / 0.77; XGBoost 0.88 / 0.87 / 0.87; LightGBM 0.84 / 0.93 / 0.88, ROC-AUC 0.998. Precision is the share of alarms that were real fraud; F1 balances Recall and precision.

Why LightGBM. Best balance (highest F1) and fewest false alarms. With only 356 fraud cases, Recall is uncertain by about ±4 points (≈ ±14 frauds), so XGBoost's 13 extra catches could be luck; the choice is a business trade-off to confirm in shadow mode.

From score to decision. The production pipeline flags a payment at a fraud probability of 0.5 or more and sends it to manual review. In the notebook, lowering the cutoff to 0.4 caught 304 instead of 299 frauds for 28 instead of 24 false alarms (highest F1, 0.884); this is a candidate change for a future release, to be confirmed on live data. Under EU payment rules (PSD2), low-risk payments may also skip extra authentication, so a good score means fewer checks for honest customers.

Launch in small steps (Lecture 8). A test score is only a promise: in the SickKids silent trial a model scored 0.90 on old data but 0.50 on new patients. Shadow mode checks the promise on NordPay's live payments without affecting customers. Analyst reviews and customer complaints give a first read within days; chargebacks complete the picture after about 60 days (Stripe faces the same delay). The canary release compares the model with today's rules in the same weeks, not before vs after.

Measure in money. On the prototype test set the champion catches 62 more frauds than the baseline; at the average fraud value of $518 that is about $32,120 less loss on 67,922 payments. Live KPIs: fraud caught, false alarms and complaints, losses prevented per month, analyst workload, results per customer group, and how often analysts overturn the model's flags (step 09 in the pipeline gives us this for every flagged payment).

Main risks. Drift: fraud patterns change, so the model ages (Lecture 8: Instacart in 2020). Wrong blocks hurt real customers. Unequal errors: age is one of the strongest signals, so error rates are checked per age group. Privacy: birth dates and home locations fall under GDPR (access by role, deletion on request, a documented model). One bad input, such as a wrong address, can corrupt several signals, so inputs are checked and rules are the fallback.

Improve every night (Lecture 2, the data engine). Every analyst review and customer complaint becomes a label. At 02:00 an AI agent retrains a candidate and compares it with the production benchmark; a data scientist signs off before release, and larger changes can still go through shadow mode first. First targets: study the 57 frauds the prototype missed, and add the behaviour signals from live data.

Benchmark: LightGBM v1.0 · Recall 84.0% · precision 92.6% · F1 0.881 · AUC 0.998

  • (~60 s) This is our AI pipeline, the same one you'll see live in a moment. It has three loops.
  • Every payment: the transaction comes in, is validated, turned into model inputs, scored by LightGBM, and the decision is made. Below a probability of 0.5 it is approved, above it is flagged as fraud.
  • During the day, people close the loop: an analyst reviews every flagged payment, and a customer complaint about an approved payment reveals a fraud we missed. Every human answer becomes a new training example.
  • Every night at 02:00 an AI agent retrains a candidate model on the new labels. Only if it beats the production benchmark, and a data scientist signs off, is it published. Otherwise we keep the current model. Let's see it live.

Source. This slide maps Ģirts' demo application ("How the AI pipeline makes a decision"); the step numbers 01–16 are the same as in the demo, so the audience can follow along.

Real-time decision (01–08). Intake: the transaction arrives from the payment system and the API validates it (all required fields present). Preprocessing: feature engineering (for example distance to the merchant and hour of the day), one-hot encoding of categories, scaling of numeric values. Model: LightGBM returns a raw score, which is turned into a fraud probability. Decision: approve below 0.5, flag as fraud (blocked for review) at 0.5 and above. In the notebook a cutoff of 0.4 caught 5 more frauds for 4 more false alarms; that is a candidate change for a future release.

Feedback loop (09–11), human in the loop. Lecture 6: people's daily work teaches the next model. Flagged payments get a manual analyst review; approved payments can be disputed by the customer, which reveals missed fraud. Both answers are stored as labelled feedback data, with the payment's features.

Nightly retraining (12–16). An AI agent (OpenClaw) retrains a candidate model on the new labels at 02:00 and compares it with the production benchmark, LightGBM v1.0 (Recall 83.99%, precision 92.57%, F1 0.8807, AUC 0.9978). If the candidate is better, a data scientist gets a review task and signs off before the model is published and deployed to step 06. If not, the current model stays. This links to the agent lectures (L10–11) and to Lecture 3's champion loop: a challenger replaces the champion only when it wins.

Good question to expect: "how much better is better?" With 356 fraud cases in the test set, Recall is uncertain by about ±4 points, so the gate should require a clear margin, not any improvement, and big changes can still go through shadow mode before full release (Lecture 8).
